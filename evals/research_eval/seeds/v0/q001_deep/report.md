# Regulatory Changes Affecting the Crypto Industry in Europe  
*Status as of October 30, 2026*

## Abstract

Europe’s crypto regulatory landscape has shifted from fragmented national registration regimes to a dense, layered system combining market licensing, stablecoin rules, anti-money laundering controls, transfer-data obligations, operational resilience, and—in some cases—payment-services regulation. The most consequential changes are not only MiCA’s entry into force, but also the end of grandfathering on 1 July 2026, the immediate enforceability of the EU Travel Rule from 30 December 2024, ESMA’s restrictive approach to reverse solicitation, the application of DORA from 17 January 2025, and the EBA’s view that some e-money token (EMT) activities can trigger PSD2. Taken together, these changes materially raise the compliance threshold for exchanges, custodians, brokers, wallet providers, stablecoin businesses, and non-EU firms serving EU users. This report argues that Europe is no longer merely “regulating crypto”; it is integrating crypto into mainstream financial regulation. The result is a more credible but also more demanding market structure that advantages authorised, well-capitalised operators and sharply reduces the viability of informal or offshore EU-facing business models. ([InnReg, 2026](https://www.innreg.com/blog/eu-crypto-regulation-guide); [ESMA, 2026a](https://www.esma.europa.eu/sites/default/files/2026-04/ESMA75-113276571-1679_Statement_on_the_end_of_transitional_periods_under_MiCA.pdf); [EBA, 2025](https://www.eba.europa.eu/sites/default/files/2025-06/e2958c99-a1b0-4b07-9d31-bcba0a28dbe7/Opinion%20on%20the%20interplay%20between%20PSD2%20and%20MiCA.pdf))

## 1. Introduction

The key regulatory change affecting the European crypto industry is that Europe now applies a **multi-layered financial-regulation model** to crypto rather than a standalone crypto rulebook. MiCA governs crypto-asset issuers and crypto-asset service providers (CASPs), the Transfer of Funds Regulation (TFR) imposes the EU crypto Travel Rule, AML laws continue to supply customer due diligence and suspicious activity reporting duties, DORA imposes operational resilience rules, and PSD2 can apply where crypto products—especially EMT-related payment flows—function like payment services. This regime applies not only to EU-incorporated firms but also to non-EU firms that target or serve EU users. ([InnReg, 2026](https://www.innreg.com/blog/eu-crypto-regulation-guide))

This shift matters because earlier debate often focused on whether crypto would be regulated at all. That question is now settled. By late 2026, the real issue is how different EU regulatory layers interact in practice: who must hold a MiCA licence, who must comply with zero-threshold Travel Rule obligations, when a stablecoin service becomes a payment service, and how far non-EU firms can rely on reverse solicitation. The deepest regulatory changes are therefore **structural**, not symbolic.

## 3. MiCA Changed the Market from National Registration to EU Authorisation

### 3.1. The end of grandfathering is the industry’s biggest 2026 shock

MiCA did not simply create a new licence. It also ended the prior world in which many firms operated under divergent national virtual-asset or AML-only registration regimes. The transitional regime under Article 143(3) was available only to CASPs that were already lawfully operating under national law before 30 December 2024, and even then only until the earlier of their national deadline, 1 July 2026, or the date their MiCA application was granted or refused. ([Deloitte Legal, n.d.](https://www.deloitte.com/dl/en/services/legal/perspectives/micar-ende-uebergangsregelung.html); [Sumsub, n.d.](https://sumsub.com/blog/crypto-regulations-in-the-european-union-markets-in-crypto-assets-mica))

ESMA’s 17 April 2026 statement removed any remaining ambiguity: after 1 July 2026, entities providing crypto-asset services to EU clients without a MiCA licence are in breach of EU law, regardless of how far local implementation had progressed in any given Member State. ESMA also expected unauthorised firms to have operational, credible, immediately executable wind-down plans already in place by that date. ([ESMA, 2026a](https://www.esma.europa.eu/sites/default/files/2026-04/ESMA75-113276571-1679_Statement_on_the_end_of_transitional_periods_under_MiCA.pdf))

The follow-up public statement of 23 June 2026 hardened the message further: unauthorised CASPs were expected to take immediate steps to wind down EU activities, notify clients, and facilitate transfer of assets either to an authorised CASP or to a self-hosted wallet. ESMA and national competent authorities (NCAs) also signaled coordinated cross-border action. ([ESMA, 2026b](https://www.esma.europa.eu/sites/default/files/2026-06/ESMA75-113276571-1710_Public_Statement_MiCA_transitional_period_ends.pdf))

A practical indication of how demanding this shift has been comes from post-transition market commentary: only about **17–20%** of the roughly **1,200** firms operating pre-MiCA reportedly secured authorisation, and ESMA’s register reportedly showed **280 authorised CASPs** by 15 July 2026. While that figure is secondary analysis rather than ESMA’s own narrative, it illustrates that the transition materially reduced the field rather than merely re-badging incumbents. ([NorthPoint, 2026](https://northpoint.fi/regulations/mica/reverse-solicitation-serving-eu-clients))

### 3.2. MiCA is not “just one law”

A common mistake is to treat MiCA as the whole EU crypto regime. It is not. MiCA supplies the authorisation and conduct framework, but other obligations still apply through parallel instruments. This is especially important for founders and non-EU firms that assume a single licence solves everything. ([InnReg, 2026](https://www.innreg.com/blog/eu-crypto-regulation-guide))

## 4. The EU Travel Rule Became an Immediate Operational Burden

### 4.1. Zero-threshold obligations changed transaction handling

From 30 December 2024, the EU’s TFR imposed the crypto Travel Rule with a **zero threshold**: originator and beneficiary data obligations apply regardless of transfer size. This is more stringent than many non-EU regimes and means even small retail transfers require compliant data handling. ([Elliptic, n.d.](https://www.elliptic.co/blockchain-basics/what-is-the-travel-rule); [InnReg, 2026](https://www.innreg.com/blog/eu-crypto-regulation-guide))

The EBA issued its Travel Rule Guidelines on 4 July 2024 because the regulation itself did not specify all information fields in sufficient detail and could otherwise have produced divergent interpretations and unnecessary rejection of compliant transfers. In other words, the regulatory change was not only legal but also technical: CASPs had to redesign messaging, data validation, and exception handling. ([EBA, 2024](https://www.eba.europa.eu/sites/default/files/2024-07/6de6e9b9-0ed9-49cd-985d-c0834b5b4356/Travel%20Rule%20Guidelines.pdf))

### 4.2. Self-hosted wallet rules are stricter than many firms expected

EU CASPs must obtain and hold the self-hosted address, ensure that each transfer can be individually identified, and assess whether the address is owned or controlled by the customer when the transfer exceeds **EUR 1,000**. These duties are separate from the zero-threshold rule: all transfers are in scope for Travel Rule purposes, but transfers above EUR 1,000 to or from self-hosted wallets trigger extra ownership/control checks. ([EBA, 2024](https://www.eba.europa.eu/sites/default/files/2024-07/6de6e9b9-0ed9-49cd-985d-c0834b5b4356/Travel%20Rule%20Guidelines.pdf))

This matters commercially. Firms that built for basic screening but not wallet verification, attribution, recordkeeping, and transfer-by-transfer identification face operational non-compliance, even if they already have MiCA ambitions. That is why several 2026 compliance analyses describe MiCA, TFR, and AML obligations as a **single compliance surface** rather than three separate workstreams. ([Zyphe, 2026](https://www.zyphe.com/resources/blog/mica-kyc-requirements-in-2026))

### 4.3. KYC duties still come from AML law, not MiCA itself

Another important nuance is that MiCA itself does not contain the full KYC rule set. Customer due diligence, ongoing monitoring, and suspicious transaction reporting continue to come primarily from AML law, while MiCA requires firms to have procedures consistent with those obligations. The result is cumulative, not substitutive, regulation. ([Zyphe, 2026](https://www.zyphe.com/resources/blog/mica-kyc-requirements-in-2026))

## 5. ESMA Narrowed Reverse Solicitation and Expanded Extraterritorial Reach

### 5.1. Non-EU firms can no longer treat the EU as “reachable by default”

ESMA’s reverse-solicitation guidelines, published on 26 February 2025 and applying 60 calendar days after publication in all EU languages, substantially narrowed the practical ability of third-country firms to serve EU clients without authorisation. ESMA states that solicitation must be construed broadly and in a technology-neutral manner. It includes promotion, advertisement, or offers through websites, apps, social media, emails, banners, pop-ups, road shows, affiliation campaigns, retargeting, messaging platforms, and sponsorship deals. Even general brand advertising with broad public reach may count. ([ESMA, 2025a](https://www.esma.europa.eu/sites/default/files/2025-02/ESMA35-1872330276-2030_Guidelines_on_reverse_solicitation_under_MiCA.pdf))

The guidelines also state that the assessment is factual and that contractual disclaimers cannot supersede contrary facts. A checkbox saying “the client approached us on their own initiative” does not save a business model that involves EU-facing marketing, search optimisation, influencer campaigns, app-store distribution, or website design pointing at EU users. ([ESMA, 2025a](https://www.esma.europa.eu/sites/default/files/2025-02/ESMA35-1872330276-2030_Guidelines_on_reverse_solicitation_under_MiCA.pdf))

### 5.2. The post-1 July 2026 environment makes this a hard enforcement issue

Once the MiCA transitional period ended on 1 July 2026, reverse solicitation stopped being a peripheral legal theory and became the last narrow exception for unlicensed non-EU firms. ESMA’s April and June 2026 statements linked reverse-solicitation abuse directly to coordinated supervisory action and wind-down expectations. ([ESMA, 2026a](https://www.esma.europa.eu/sites/default/files/2026-04/ESMA75-113276571-1679_Statement_on_the_end_of_transitional_periods_under_MiCA.pdf); [ESMA, 2026b](https://www.esma.europa.eu/sites/default/files/2026-06/ESMA75-113276571-1710_Public_Statement_MiCA_transitional_period_ends.pdf))

**Analytical view:** reverse solicitation is no longer a viable EU market-access strategy for any firm with meaningful EU revenue. It survives only for genuinely incidental, client-initiated cases.

## 6. Stablecoins Are Regulated Earlier and More Deeply Than Many Firms Realised

### 6.1. Stablecoin rules did not begin in July 2026

A persistent market misunderstanding was that 1 July 2026 marked the start of European stablecoin regulation. It did not. The stablecoin provisions for asset-referenced tokens (ARTs) and e-money tokens (EMTs) became applicable on **30 June 2024**. The July 2026 date mainly ended grandfathering for CASPs. ([InnReg, 2026](https://www.innreg.com/blog/eu-crypto-regulation-guide); [0xProcessing, 2026](https://0xprocessing.com/blog/mica-regulation-crypto-payments-eu))

ESMA and the European Commission reinforced this in January 2025, stating that NCAs should ensure CASP compliance regarding non-MiCA-compliant ARTs and EMTs as soon as possible and no later than the end of **Q1 2025**. The Commission also clarified that some crypto-asset services can amount to an offer to the public or an admission to trading, bringing them within MiCA Titles III and IV. ([ESMA, 2025b](https://www.esma.europa.eu/press-news/esma-news/esma-and-european-commission-publish-guidance-non-mica-compliant-arts-and-emts))

### 6.2. EMTs are where MiCA and payment law intersect

The EBA’s 2025 opinion is one of the most important deeper regulatory developments. EMTs have a dual legal nature: under MiCA they are crypto-assets, but MiCA Article 48(2) also deems them electronic money, meaning they are treated as “funds” for PSD2 purposes. This creates a genuine overlap between crypto regulation and payment regulation. ([EBA, 2025](https://www.eba.europa.eu/sites/default/files/2025-06/e2958c99-a1b0-4b07-9d31-bcba0a28dbe7/Opinion%20on%20the%20interplay%20between%20PSD2%20and%20MiCA.pdf))

The EBA’s interpretation is that certain EMT activities are payment services under PSD2—especially transfers of EMTs on behalf of clients and, in some cases, custody/administration where the custodial wallet allows sending to and receiving from third parties. By contrast, exchange of crypto-assets for funds and exchange of crypto-assets for other crypto-assets is not treated as a payment service merely because it involves EMTs. ([EBA, 2025](https://www.eba.europa.eu/sites/default/files/2025-06/e2958c99-a1b0-4b07-9d31-bcba0a28dbe7/Opinion%20on%20the%20interplay%20between%20PSD2%20and%20MiCA.pdf); [DLA Piper, 2025](https://www.dlapiperintelligence.com/investmentrules/blog/articles/2025/interplay-between-mica-and-psd2-for-transfers-of-electronic-money-tokens))

This is strategically important. It means some “crypto payments” businesses may need more than MiCA authorisation: they may also need PSD2/EMI structuring, licensing, or partnerships. The EU is therefore not merely regulating stablecoins as tokens; it is regulating them as payment instruments where functionally appropriate.

## 7. DORA Added a New Compliance Layer Beyond Financial Crime

Since 17 January 2025, DORA has applied to financial entities regulated under EU law, including MiCA-licensed crypto firms. DORA requires ICT risk identification, business continuity and disaster recovery planning, testing, and oversight of third-party technology providers, especially cloud and data vendors. ([InnReg, 2026](https://www.innreg.com/blog/eu-crypto-regulation-guide))

This is a significant change because earlier European crypto regulation was often discussed primarily through AML and market-conduct lenses. DORA makes operational resilience a first-order supervisory issue. For CASPs, compliance is no longer limited to licensing files and AML manuals; it extends into architecture, vendor governance, incident processes, and testing discipline.

---

## 8. Scope Boundaries Still Matter: MiFID II, DeFi, and NFTs

MiCA is a **residual regime**, not a universal one. If a token qualifies as a financial instrument under MiFID II, it falls outside MiCA’s crypto-asset perimeter and into the existing securities regime. ESMA’s 19 March 2025 guidelines were designed to improve common, consistent assessment of when crypto-assets should qualify as financial instruments. ([ESMA, 2025c](https://www.esma.europa.eu/sites/default/files/2025-03/ESMA75453128700-1323_Guidelines_on_the_conditions_and_criteria_for_the_qualification_of_CAs_as_FIs.pdf))

This affects tokenized real-world assets, structured products, and some yield-bearing or investment-like tokens. It also shows why Europe’s regulatory development is deeper than a single crypto rulebook: classification can move a product from MiCA into MiFID II, while EMT functionality can move it toward PSD2.

For DeFi and NFTs, the perimeter remains more conditional. Truly decentralised protocols without an identifiable operator remain generally outside MiCA, but firms that build, maintain, or market DeFi interfaces may still be pulled into scope. NFTs are generally excluded unless fractionalised or structured in a way that resembles financial instruments. ([InnReg, 2026](https://www.innreg.com/blog/eu-crypto-regulation-guide))

## 10. Conclusion

The regulatory changes affecting the crypto industry in Europe are not isolated rule updates; they amount to a **reconstruction of the industry’s legal operating model**. The deepest change is the end of regulatory ambiguity. Europe has now combined MiCA, TFR, AML, DORA, and—in relevant cases—PSD2 into a coherent supervisory perimeter.

My concrete assessment is that **the most important change is not MiCA alone, but the closure of regulatory arbitrage between crypto, payments, and cross-border digital distribution**. The July 2026 end of grandfathering removed the old national-regime safety net. The Travel Rule made transfer compliance operationally unavoidable. ESMA’s reverse-solicitation guidance severely restricted offshore workarounds. The EBA’s EMT/PSD2 analysis ensured that stablecoin payment models cannot assume MiCA is their only legal burden.

The likely market result is durable: Europe is becoming a jurisdiction where crypto businesses increasingly resemble regulated financial institutions. That is positive for legal certainty, consumer protection, and supervisory consistency. It is also costly. In practice, the new regime favours authorised, well-funded operators with strong compliance, payments, and technology capabilities, while making life materially harder for small exchanges, lightly governed wallet products, and offshore firms hoping to serve EU users without full authorisation.
