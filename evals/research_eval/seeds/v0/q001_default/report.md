# Regulatory Changes Affecting the Crypto Industry in Europe

## Executive Summary

The European crypto industry is being reshaped by a **stack of overlapping regulatory changes**, led by the EU’s Markets in Crypto-Assets Regulation (**MiCA**), but extending well beyond it into **AML/KYC**, the **Travel Rule**, **operational resilience**, **payments/e-money law**, and **tax reporting**. The single biggest change is that Europe has moved from a fragmented, nationally uneven environment to a **licence-based, pan-EU supervisory regime** that is much closer to traditional financial regulation than the crypto sector previously experienced. In practical terms, this means that access to the European market is increasingly determined by a firm’s ability to obtain authorization, document governance, manage AML risk, handle customer data, and maintain resilient technology infrastructure rather than by speed of market entry alone ([Norton Rose Fulbright, 2026](https://www.nortonrosefulbright.com/en/knowledge/publications/2cec201e/regulating-crypto-assets-in-europe-practical-guide-to-mica); [Sumsub, 2026](https://sumsub.com/blog/crypto-regulations-in-the-european-union-markets-in-crypto-assets-mica)).

My assessment is that **MiCA is the anchor regulation, but not the whole story**. The most consequential development for crypto businesses is the combination of:  
1. **MiCA authorization and passporting rules**,  
2. **the expiry of transitional/grandfathering periods**,  
3. **stablecoin-specific constraints**,  
4. **full Travel Rule data-sharing obligations**,  
5. **DORA-style ICT and third-party risk controls**, and  
6. **DAC8 tax transparency reporting**.  

Together, these rules reduce regulatory arbitrage and increase legal certainty, but they also **raise fixed compliance costs** and are likely to favor larger, better-capitalized operators over smaller or offshore firms ([Unit21, 2026](https://www.unit21.ai/blog/mica-regulation-2026-faqs-what-crypto-compliance-teams-need-to-know); [CoinTracking, 2026](https://cointracking.info/learn/dac8-crypto-regulation)).

## The Core Regulatory Shift: MiCA

### Europe’s first comprehensive crypto rulebook

MiCA, formally **Regulation (EU) 2023/1114**, is the EU’s first comprehensive framework for crypto-assets and crypto-asset service providers (**CASPs**). It creates harmonized rules across EU member states for crypto-assets not already covered by existing EU financial services law, replacing the earlier patchwork of national regimes that created uncertainty and encouraged regulatory arbitrage ([Norton Rose Fulbright, 2026](https://www.nortonrosefulbright.com/en/knowledge/publications/2cec201e/regulating-crypto-assets-in-europe-practical-guide-to-mica); [Sumsub, 2026](https://sumsub.com/blog/crypto-regulations-in-the-european-union-markets-in-crypto-assets-mica)).

MiCA covers the full lifecycle of many crypto activities, including **issuance**, **offering to the public**, **admission to trading**, **crypto-asset services**, and **market abuse prevention**. The regulation is therefore not just a licensing regime; it is also a conduct, disclosure, and market integrity framework ([Norton Rose Fulbright, 2026](https://www.nortonrosefulbright.com/en/knowledge/publications/2cec201e/regulating-crypto-assets-in-europe-practical-guide-to-mica); [Sumsub, 2026](https://sumsub.com/blog/crypto-regulations-in-the-european-union-markets-in-crypto-assets-mica)).

### Key implementation milestones

MiCA has rolled out in phases, with different obligations becoming applicable on different dates:

| Date | Regulatory change | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| 29 June 2023 | MiCA entered into force | Became part of EU law ([StealthEX, 2026](https://stealthex.io/blog/mica-compliant-stablecoins)) |
| 30 June 2024 | Rules for asset-referenced tokens (ARTs) and e-money tokens (EMTs) became applicable | Stablecoins became subject to dedicated issuer rules ([Norton Rose Fulbright, 2026](https://www.nortonrosefulbright.com/en/knowledge/publications/2cec201e/regulating-crypto-assets-in-europe-practical-guide-to-mica); [Sumsub, 2026](https://sumsub.com/blog/crypto-regulations-in-the-european-union-markets-in-crypto-assets-mica)) |
| 30 December 2024 | Full MiCA framework for CASPs took effect | Exchanges, custodians, advisers, and other service providers came under EU-wide authorization rules ([Norton Rose Fulbright, 2026](https://www.nortonrosefulbright.com/en/knowledge/publications/2cec201e/regulating-crypto-assets-in-europe-practical-guide-to-mica); [Sumsub, 2026](https://sumsub.com/blog/crypto-regulations-in-the-european-union-markets-in-crypto-assets-mica)) |
| 1 July 2026 | Maximum transitional period expired | Grandfathered firms could no longer rely on Article 143 beyond this date ([Sumsub, 2026](https://sumsub.com/blog/crypto-regulations-in-the-european-union-markets-in-crypto-assets-mica); [StealthEX, 2026](https://stealthex.io/blog/mica-compliant-stablecoins)) |

This timeline matters because it marks the transition from **preparation** to **enforcement**. As of late 2026, MiCA is no longer a future compliance project; it is a live operational reality for firms serving EU clients ([Hacken, 2026](https://hacken.io/discover/mica-regulation)).

## Who Is Affected

MiCA is relevant to both **issuers of crypto-assets** and **CASPs** operating in Europe or serving European clients. Norton Rose specifically notes that the framework is relevant not only to persons located or established in the EEA, but also to persons established **outside the EEA**, including in the UK, if they have clients located in the EEA ([Norton Rose Fulbright, 2026](https://www.nortonrosefulbright.com/en/knowledge/publications/2cec201e/regulating-crypto-assets-in-europe-practical-guide-to-mica)).

CASPs include firms such as exchanges, trading platforms, custodians, transfer service providers, order execution businesses, portfolio managers, and advisers focused on crypto-assets ([Cyfrin, 2026](https://www.cyfrin.io/blog/mica-regulation-explained-a-guide-to-eu-crypto-compliance)).

At the same time, MiCA does **not** apply to every crypto-related activity. Sources indicate that important areas outside the current perimeter include:  
- crypto-assets that qualify as **financial instruments** under MiFID II,  
- genuinely unique **NFTs**,  
- central banks and public institutions, and  
- protocols without a central operator or control structure, at least for now ([Norton Rose Fulbright, 2026](https://www.nortonrosefulbright.com/en/knowledge/publications/2cec201e/regulating-crypto-assets-in-europe-practical-guide-to-mica); [InnReg, 2026](https://www.innreg.com/blog/eu-crypto-regulation-guide)).

## Authorization, Passporting, and the End of Regulatory Arbitrage

### MiCA authorization is now central

Any person that continues to provide crypto-asset services in Europe after 30 December 2024 must assess whether MiCA authorization is required. Norton Rose warns that preparing the required documentation, policies, and procedures can be a **lengthy process**, especially for firms with complex operations or no existing European presence. It also warns of potential **application bottlenecks** at national competent authorities (**NCAs**) as deadlines approach ([Norton Rose Fulbright, 2026](https://www.nortonrosefulbright.com/en/knowledge/publications/2cec201e/regulating-crypto-assets-in-europe-practical-guide-to-mica)).

This is a major departure from the previous environment, where firms could often operate under lighter local registrations or exploit divergence among member states. Unit21 characterizes MiCA as ending the era of **intra-European regulatory arbitrage** ([Unit21, 2026](https://www.unit21.ai/blog/mica-regulation-2026-faqs-what-crypto-compliance-teams-need-to-know)).

### Transitional relief is over, and grandfathering had limits

MiCA allowed a transitional period under which some firms that had been operating legally under national law before 30 December 2024 could continue temporarily until **1 July 2026**, or until they received or were refused authorization, whichever came first. However, member states were permitted to shorten or not use that transitional period ([Sumsub, 2026](https://sumsub.com/blog/crypto-regulations-in-the-european-union-markets-in-crypto-assets-mica); [StealthEX, 2026](https://stealthex.io/blog/mica-compliant-stablecoins)).

Crucially, Norton Rose stresses that **grandfathering did not make a firm a MiCA-authorized CASP**. One practical consequence is that grandfathered firms could **not benefit from intra-EU passporting** ([Norton Rose Fulbright, 2026](https://www.nortonrosefulbright.com/en/knowledge/publications/2cec201e/regulating-crypto-assets-in-europe-practical-guide-to-mica)).

### Passporting is a strategic advantage

Once authorized, a CASP can provide services **throughout the Union** without setting up a physical presence in every member state. Didit notes that a CASP may begin in a host state once the home authority communicates the notification, or at the latest on the **15th calendar day** after the notice is submitted ([Didit, 2026](https://didit.me/blog/mica-regulation-eu-crypto-compliance-guide)).

The regulatory effect is clear: **authorization has become a distribution right**. In my view, this is one of MiCA’s most commercially significant features because it turns compliance into a market access asset.

## Stablecoins: The Tightest Immediate Change

### EMTs and ARTs are under dedicated regulation

The most visible early impact of MiCA has been on stablecoins. Since **30 June 2024**, ARTs and EMTs have been subject to a dedicated regime covering authorization, reserves or safeguarded funds, governance, disclosures, and redemption rights ([Norton Rose Fulbright, 2026](https://www.nortonrosefulbright.com/en/knowledge/publications/2cec201e/regulating-crypto-assets-in-europe-practical-guide-to-mica); [StealthEX, 2026](https://stealthex.io/blog/mica-compliant-stablecoins)).

Didit summarizes a particularly important rule: an **e-money token may be offered to the public in the EU only by an issuer authorized as a credit institution or e-money institution**, after notifying and publishing a white paper, or by others with that issuer’s written consent ([Didit, 2026](https://didit.me/blog/mica-regulation-eu-crypto-compliance-guide)).

### Market impact: restrictions and delistings

These rules have had immediate commercial consequences. Industry reporting indicates that platforms have restricted or delisted non-MiCA-compliant stablecoins for EEA users. StealthEX cites examples involving **Binance**, **Coinbase**, and **Kraken** limiting USDT-related services in parts of Europe, while emphasizing that these are often **platform-level compliance decisions** rather than blanket EU bans on private possession ([StealthEX, 2026](https://stealthex.io/blog/mica-compliant-stablecoins)).

Didit also reports that ESMA stated in January 2025 that trading platforms were expected to stop offering trading in EMTs where the issuer was not authorized in the EU, with a temporary **“sell only”** window until the end of the first quarter of 2025 while custody and transfers could continue ([Didit, 2026](https://didit.me/blog/mica-regulation-eu-crypto-compliance-guide)).

This distinction matters. MiCA is **not primarily criminalizing private ownership of tokens in self-custody**; it is regulating **issuance, offering, trading admission, and service provision** around those tokens ([StealthEX, 2026](https://stealthex.io/blog/mica-compliant-stablecoins)).

## AML/KYC and the Travel Rule: Compliance Has Become Data-Intensive

MiCA is often treated as the “crypto licence,” but it does **not itself contain the full KYC rulebook**. Didit notes that KYC obligations come from the wider EU AML framework, the **Transfer of Funds Regulation**, and other adjacent laws ([Didit, 2026](https://didit.me/blog/mica-regulation-eu-crypto-compliance-guide)).

A major current change is that, since **December 2024**, CASPs have been required to collect **full originator and beneficiary information for every crypto transfer**, with **no minimum threshold**. Unit21 specifies that this includes name, account number, address or national ID, and date of birth, with the data needing to be shared with the receiving CASP ([Unit21, 2026](https://www.unit21.ai/blog/mica-regulation-2026-faqs-what-crypto-compliance-teams-need-to-know)).

This is operationally significant because it turns compliance into a **data exchange architecture problem**, not just a policy problem. Firms need systems that can ingest, validate, transmit, and store Travel Rule data for every transfer, and detect missing or inconsistent information ([Unit21, 2026](https://www.unit21.ai/blog/mica-regulation-2026-faqs-what-crypto-compliance-teams-need-to-know)).

MiCA authorization applications also require applicants to describe the controls, policies, and procedures they will use to identify, assess, and manage **money laundering and terrorist financing risks** ([Didit, 2026](https://didit.me/blog/mica-regulation-eu-crypto-compliance-guide)).

## DORA and Operational Resilience: Technology Risk Is Now a Regulatory Issue

Europe’s crypto regulatory changes are not limited to financial conduct. Firms also face increasing operational resilience expectations. InnReg states that the EU’s Digital Operational Resilience Act (**DORA**) requires firms to monitor critical third-party providers, especially **cloud and data vendors**, and to report major ICT incidents promptly, including root causes and remediation plans. It also emphasizes that structured documentation, internal controls, and repeatable testing are necessary to comply ([InnReg, 2026](https://www.innreg.com/blog/eu-crypto-regulation-guide)).

Unit21 similarly advises compliance teams to review DORA compliance because ICT risk management, incident reporting, and third-party oversight are now regulatory requirements, not optional good practice ([Unit21, 2026](https://www.unit21.ai/blog/mica-regulation-2026-faqs-what-crypto-compliance-teams-need-to-know)).

The practical consequence is that crypto firms can no longer treat infrastructure choices as purely technical or commercial matters. Vendor management and cyber resilience have become **supervisory topics**.

## Overlap With Payments and E-Money Law

MiCA does not replace existing **payments** or **e-money** law. InnReg notes that if a crypto product falls within those categories, firms may need to consider **dual licensing or structural changes** ([InnReg, 2026](https://www.innreg.com/blog/eu-crypto-regulation-guide)).

Industry sources report that, from **March 2026**, CASPs offering custody and transfer services for EMTs may in some cases need both **MiCA authorization** and a separate **PSD2 payment services licence**, increasing cost and complexity ([Unit21, 2026](https://www.unit21.ai/blog/mica-regulation-2026-faqs-what-crypto-compliance-teams-need-to-know); [Cyfrin, 2026](https://www.cyfrin.io/blog/mica-regulation-explained-a-guide-to-eu-crypto-compliance)).

Even where legal interpretation remains fact-specific, the trend is unmistakable: **stablecoin-related activities are being pulled closer to conventional payment regulation**.

## Tax Transparency: DAC8 Is Expanding the Reporting Burden

A separate but important regulatory change is **DAC8**, the EU framework for tax reporting on crypto-assets. CoinTracking reports that, although some member states missed the transposition deadline, the European Commission sent formal letters in **February 2026** to **12 countries**—Belgium, Bulgaria, Cyprus, Czechia, Estonia, Greece, Luxembourg, Malta, the Netherlands, Poland, Portugal, and Spain—giving them two months to complete transposition ([CoinTracking, 2026](https://cointracking.info/learn/dac8-crypto-regulation)).

For traders and firms, CoinTracking says the practical effect is that **2026 transaction data is still being collected by CASPs**, and the first exchange deadline is **30 September 2027** ([CoinTracking, 2026](https://cointracking.info/learn/dac8-crypto-regulation)).

This is another indication that Europe is moving crypto into a standard financial transparency model: not only licensed and supervised, but also systematically reportable to tax authorities.

## Enforcement Risk and Market Consequences

MiCA’s enforcement architecture is meaningful. Didit states that, for breaches of CASP rules, supervisors must be able to impose maximum fines on a company of at least **EUR 5,000,000** and at least **5% of total annual turnover**, while for ART and EMT rules the turnover floor is **12.5%**, with member states free to set higher levels ([Didit, 2026](https://didit.me/blog/mica-regulation-eu-crypto-compliance-guide)).

Norton Rose also notes that providers operating without the required authorization may be placed on a **register of non-compliant CASPs** ([Norton Rose Fulbright, 2026](https://www.nortonrosefulbright.com/en/knowledge/publications/2cec201e/regulating-crypto-assets-in-europe-practical-guide-to-mica)).

Media coverage suggests the licensing transition has been difficult in practice. Yahoo Finance, citing a Euronews report, said that **fewer than 1 in 5 of Europe’s 1,200 registered crypto companies** had secured a licence when the new rules took full effect, illustrating market strain even if that figure should be treated as journalistic rather than official supervisory data ([Yahoo Finance, 2026](https://finance.yahoo.com/markets/crypto/articles/watch-brand-european-crypto-regulations-070110178.html)).

## Future Regulatory Pressure: DeFi, NFTs, Offshore Firms, and Secondary Rules

Europe’s regulatory framework is still evolving. Norton Rose notes that the European Commission is **still adopting secondary legislation** that sets technical and operational details for MiCA, meaning firms must track not only the core regulation but also implementing measures ([Norton Rose Fulbright, 2026](https://www.nortonrosefulbright.com/en/knowledge/publications/2cec201e/regulating-crypto-assets-in-europe-practical-guide-to-mica)).

Several sources also indicate that **DeFi**, **NFTs**, **AI-enabled crypto platforms**, and **algorithmic or hybrid token models** may face future rule changes. InnReg and Cyfrin both suggest European regulators are considering whether ambiguous or hybrid arrangements should face additional oversight ([InnReg, 2026](https://www.innreg.com/blog/eu-crypto-regulation-guide); [Cyfrin, 2026](https://www.cyfrin.io/blog/mica-regulation-explained-a-guide-to-eu-crypto-compliance)).

There is also pressure around the treatment of **foreign or offshore token issuers**. Yahoo Finance reported that EU diplomats had already acknowledged potential gaps regarding tokens created outside Europe and that amendments may be needed ([Yahoo Finance, 2026](https://finance.yahoo.com/markets/crypto/articles/watch-brand-european-crypto-regulations-070110178.html)).

## Conclusion

The regulatory changes affecting the crypto industry in Europe are **broad, structural, and cumulative**. MiCA has transformed crypto from a fragmented market governed largely by national registration, informal practice, and legal uncertainty into a more standardized and highly supervised financial services sector. But the deeper change is the **layering effect**: MiCA licensing, stablecoin restrictions, Travel Rule data-sharing, DORA resilience obligations, tax reporting under DAC8, and possible overlap with PSD2 and AML reform.

My concrete view is that **Europe is not merely regulating crypto more strictly; it is normalizing crypto into the architecture of mainstream financial regulation**. This is good for legal certainty, investor protection, and institutional adoption, but it also raises barriers to entry and narrows the viability of lightly regulated or offshore operating models. For crypto firms, the European market is still attractive because MiCA offers passportable access across the EU. However, that access now comes at the price of serious compliance maturity. In 2026, the central competitive question in Europe is no longer “Can you launch?” but “Can you remain authorized, data-compliant, operationally resilient, and tax-reportable at scale?” ([Norton Rose Fulbright, 2026](https://www.nortonrosefulbright.com/en/knowledge/publications/2cec201e/regulating-crypto-assets-in-europe-practical-guide-to-mica); [Unit21, 2026](https://www.unit21.ai/blog/mica-regulation-2026-faqs-what-crypto-compliance-teams-need-to-know); [CoinTracking, 2026](https://cointracking.info/learn/dac8-crypto-regulation)).
